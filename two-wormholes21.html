How the connected Ellis wormhole works

Geometry and coordinates

The room display is 10 m × 10 m × 30 m, spanning z = −15 m to +15 m, with red, green, and blue 10 m sections. The geometry is one symmetric, massless Ellis bridge (m = 0), with spatial metric dl² + (l² + a²)dΩ² and signed proper-radial coordinate l. Its two asymptotically flat charts are displayed with their mouths at z = −10 m and z = +10 m.

Massless means the wormhole's gravitational mass, measured far away in either region, is zero. It does not mean the throat or curvature is absent: the throat radius a is nonzero, and the geometry is supported by an exotic scalar field. In this model there is no mass-related gravitational redshift; the time component of the metric is constant.

The areal radius is r = √(l² + a²). It has its minimum value a at the throat. Crossing the throat changes to the paired room chart at the other mouth; the displayed z coordinate changes from one mouth location to the other.

Camera movement

W/S, A/D, and the vertical controls move the camera through the room display. Crossing the circular throat aperture maps it to the paired mouth.

How the image is rendered

A separate light ray is traced for every screen pixel through the Ellis spatial metric, starting at the camera's location. The fourth-order Runge–Kutta integrator follows the geodesic throughout the chart; there is no aperture-triggered switch to a straight ray. The Ellis curvature therefore affects rays at every distance and falls away smoothly toward the asymptotically flat region. Wall color is selected from displayed z: red for z < −5 m, green for −5 m < z < +5 m, and blue for z > +5 m.

This is the exact single Ellis metric on its two charts. The colored 30 m room is a visual coordinate layout for those charts, not a separate numerical solution of the Einstein equations for a wormhole embedded in one ordinary room.

An STL is rendered as a surface in this ray-traced scene. Its position slider moves it along the signed radial coordinate l, through the throat. The current mapping keeps its local orientation fixed; this is not a simulation of a freely moving rigid body or of tidal forces on the object.

Controls and scope

Ray steps sets the maximum integration steps per ray. The control ranges from 300 to 500 and starts at 400. More steps let a ray travel farther before stopping, at the cost of rendering time. Wall grid line thickness changes the drawn grid width; it is a visual setting. The wall shading and grid are illustrative. This is a static ray-path visualization, not a complete simulation of materials, reflected light, or time-dependent gravitational physics.
